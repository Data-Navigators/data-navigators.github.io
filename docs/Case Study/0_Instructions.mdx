Welcome to the Case Study section of our data analysis course! Here, you will apply the concepts and techniques you have learned to solve real-world business problems using public or simulated datasets.

Each case study is accompanied by a business context and a decision-making situation. You are expected to define a focused problem statement rather than answer a fixed list of questions. Your analysis may use data cleaning, visualization, descriptive statistics, statistical modeling, data modeling, and actionable recommendations.

This section is not only about finding solutions but also about understanding the process of data-driven decision-making. We encourage you to think critically, explore multiple approaches, and leverage the power of data to uncover meaningful insights.

## Where the case study fits in the course

The case study is the point where the modules come together. The expected flow is:

```text
Business understanding
        ↓
Raw data and source-system understanding
        ↓
Data processing and preparation
        ↓
Exploratory data analysis
        ↓
Statistical and descriptive analysis
        ↓
Data modeling, when the problem benefits from a reusable model
        ↓
Decision, recommendation, and stakeholder communication
```

In the **Data Processing** module, raw data is taken through an ETL or ELT-style process. The result is a cleaner, documented dataset that an analyst or data scientist can consume. This work may be owned by a data engineer or analytics engineer in a real organization, but an analyst should still understand the transformation logic, assumptions, and limitations.

The case study then uses the prepared data to move from **descriptive analysis** (what happened), to **diagnostic analysis** (why it may have happened), and, where the evidence supports it, to **prescriptive thinking** (what the business could do next). Statistical concepts, exploratory analysis, visualizations, tables, metrics, and KPIs help establish what the data can and cannot support.

Data modeling is one possible route after the data has been understood. Use it when the problem requires reusable relationships across customers, products, orders, payments, sellers, or other entities. A model is not mandatory for every analysis. For a focused question, a validated consolidated table may be sufficient if its grain and limitations are clear.

### Two valid analysis routes

**Model-driven route**

```text
Prepared data
    ↓
Define grain, entities, keys, and relationships
    ↓
Create a relational or analytical model
    ↓
Validate joins and metric reconciliation
    ↓
Analyze and communicate findings
```

**Consolidated-analysis route**

```text
Prepared data
    ↓
Select a safe, documented analytical table
    ↓
Define metrics and KPIs
    ↓
Test hypotheses with statistics and visual evidence
    ↓
Analyze and communicate findings
```

Both routes are acceptable when they are justified. In either route, document the grain, cleaning decisions, assumptions, metric definitions, and limitations. Do not create a model or a dashboard simply because the tool makes it possible.

## How many case studies should I attempt?
You are required to solve only one case study for successful completion of this course. **Case Study 1 is the main and recommended case study.** Case Studies 2 and 3 are optional alternatives for learners who want a smaller or more focused dataset. Please pair with a fellow learner if your course format supports collaborative work.

### Choosing a case study

- Choose **Case Study 1** when you want the full consultant experience: multiple related files, more complex data preparation, grain and relationship decisions, deeper exploration, and a richer stakeholder problem.
- Choose **Case Study 2 or Case Study 3** when you need an optional alternative with a narrower business context or a smaller analytical scope.
- Case Study 1 contains many tables, so understanding the file structure, keys, grains, and relationships can take time. That complexity is intentional and is part of the learning experience.
- You do not have to build the Case Study 1 data model from scratch. You may use a standard, well-documented modeling pattern or existing code as a starting point, then inspect it, adapt it, validate it, and explain why it is appropriate for your problem statement.
- If a full data model is not necessary for your selected question, use a safe consolidated analytical table instead. Document its grain, transformations, assumptions, and limitations.

## Which environment do I use for doing analysis?
The datasets are shared on Kaggle. You can use Kaggle as a platform to create a notebook and solve the case study, or use your local development environment if you are comfortable doing so.

## How do I share the results of my analysis?
You will get a chance to showcase your analysis to your trainers in a 30 min session. Think of this as a session to practice your data storytelling skills.
You will also be required to share the presented notebook with the trainers for detailed evaluation and feedback.

## What are the expected Outcomes from my case study analysis?

1. Define one decision-focused problem statement from the selected case study and produce a presentable analysis that you can explain in 30 minutes. Do not treat the investigation directions as a fixed question list.
2. Present your results to the relevant stakeholder group, such as sales, marketing, operations, merchandising, or leadership. Use simplified and business-relevant storytelling.
3. The Panel will use 15 min to ask you technical questions after the presentation and provide feedback. This will be the time to showcase your learning and technical prowess.
4. Use visualizations, tables, or statistical evidence where they help the stakeholder understand the decision.
5. Use data to support your recommendations while also putting your recommendations as statements to ensure a non-data person can understand them.
6. Choose metrics that fit the domain. Define each metric, explain its calculation, document assumptions, and confirm that the metric supports the decision you are discussing.

7. Show the evidence behind the recommendation using an appropriate combination of visuals, tables, numbers, metrics, KPIs, statistical results, or model outputs.

## A sample content structure for sharing Analysis:

1. Problem statement and stakeholder decision
2. Data understanding and data grain
3. Cleaning and processing steps and their impact
4. Assumptions and limitations
5. Key findings and supporting evidence
6. Recommendation and possible next steps

Feel free to modify this based on your need, but try to keep the elements which will help the relevant stakeholder understand your approach and outcomes.

## How to present the outcome

There is no single required presentation format. Choose the format that communicates the decision clearly, such as:

- An executive dashboard.
- A presentation deck.
- A written analytical report.
- A notebook with an executive summary.
- A recorded or live video presentation.

Whatever format you choose, present the work as a consultant: explain the original decision, show how the data supports or limits the conclusion, state the recommendation, and identify what should happen next. The final product may be a dashboard, but the dashboard is only one way to communicate the analysis; it is not the analysis itself.
