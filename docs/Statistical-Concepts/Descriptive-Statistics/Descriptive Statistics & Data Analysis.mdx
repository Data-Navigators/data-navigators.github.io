# Descriptive Statistics: Understanding What the Data Is Telling You

In the previous session, we learned how to think about data.

We learned about:

* Data
* Observations
* Variables
* Population and sample
* Data quality
* Sampling
* Sampling error and bias
* Parameters and statistics
* Confidence intervals
* Statistical significance

Now imagine that your dataset has passed all the basic quality checks.

You have thousands or millions of observations.

The next question is:

> **How do you quickly understand what is happening in all that data?**

This is where **descriptive statistics** becomes useful.

---

# 1. What Are Descriptive Statistics?

Descriptive statistics are techniques used to **summarize, organize, and understand the main characteristics of a dataset**.

Instead of looking at thousands of individual observations, we can use a few meaningful measures to answer questions such as:

* What is a typical value?
* How much do the values vary?
* Are the values concentrated or widely spread?
* Is the data symmetric or skewed?
* Are there unusual values?
* Are there different groups or patterns?
* Are two variables related?

For example, imagine a food delivery company has recorded the delivery time for **100,000 orders**.

Looking at every delivery time individually is difficult.

Instead, we might summarize the data as:

> Average delivery time: **38 minutes**
> Median delivery time: **34 minutes**
> Minimum: **18 minutes**
> Maximum: **120 minutes**
> Standard deviation: **15 minutes**

These numbers give us a quick understanding of the dataset.

But there is an important principle:

> **A summary statistic is useful only when we understand what it is telling us — and what it is hiding.**

---

# 2. A Business Example: Food Delivery Performance

Let's use a simple consulting scenario throughout this module.

You are working with a food delivery company.

The business wants to understand:

> **"How long does it typically take us to deliver an order, and how consistent is our delivery experience?"**

The company provides you with delivery-time data for several thousand orders.

Your job is to help answer:

1. What is a typical delivery time?
2. How much do delivery times vary?
3. Are there unusually long deliveries?
4. Is delivery time consistent?
5. Is the data symmetric or skewed?
6. Are some regions performing differently?
7. Does distance from the restaurant relate to delivery time?

These questions introduce the major concepts in descriptive statistics.

---

# 3. The Three Questions You Should Ask First

When you receive numerical data, start with three simple questions:

### 1. Where is the center?

> What is a typical value?

This leads to:

* Mean
* Median
* Mode

### 2. How spread out is the data?

> How much do observations differ from each other?

This leads to:

* Range
* IQR
* Variance
* Standard deviation

### 3. What does the overall shape look like?

> How are the values distributed?

This leads to:

* Symmetry
* Skewness
* Outliers
* Peaks or clusters

Later, we can also ask:

> **Are variables related to each other?**

This leads to:

* Correlation
* Association

---

# 4. Measures of Central Tendency

Suppose five customers experienced the following delivery times:

```text
20, 25, 30, 35, 40 minutes
```

We want one number that represents the "center" of the data.

Three commonly used measures are:

* Mean
* Median
* Mode

---

# 5. Mean

The **mean** is what we commonly call the average.

It is calculated as:

> **Mean = Sum of all values ÷ Number of values**

For our example:

```text
20 + 25 + 30 + 35 + 40
-----------------------
          5
```

The mean is:

**30 minutes**

The mean can be thought of as the **balance point** or **center of gravity** of the data.

### Business interpretation

If the average delivery time is 30 minutes, we can say:

> "Across these observations, the average delivery time is 30 minutes."

But be careful.

The mean does **not necessarily represent the delivery time experienced by a typical customer**.

Why?

Because extreme values can strongly influence it.

---

# 6. Median

The **median** is the middle value when observations are arranged in order.

Consider:

```text
20, 25, 30, 35, 40
```

The middle value is:

**30**

Therefore:

**Median = 30 minutes**

The median is useful because it is relatively resistant to extreme values.

---

# 7. Mean, Median, and Distribution Shape

Now imagine one delivery took extremely long:

```text
20, 25, 30, 35, 120
```

The mean becomes:

```text
20 + 25 + 30 + 35 + 120
------------------------
           5
```

The mean is now:

**46 minutes**

But the median is still:

**30 minutes**

Notice what happened.

The extreme delivery of 120 minutes pulled the mean upward.

The median remained much more stable.

This is why:

> **Mean is sensitive to extreme values.**

and:

> **Median is more resistant to extreme values.**

The shape of the distribution helps us understand why.

# 8. Mode

The **mode** is the most frequently occurring value.

Suppose delivery times are:

```text
25, 30, 30, 30, 35, 40
```

The mode is:

**30 minutes**

because 30 occurs most frequently.

The mode is particularly useful when we want to know:

> **What happens most often?**

---

# 9. Mode Is Useful for Categorical Data

Mode does not require numerical data.

Suppose customers use these payment methods:

```text
UPI
Card
UPI
Cash
UPI
Card
UPI
```

The mode is:

**UPI**

because it occurs most frequently.

This makes mode particularly useful for questions such as:

* What is the most common payment method?
* What is the most common product size?
* Which category has the most customers?
* What is the most frequently reported complaint?

---

# 10. Mean, Median and Mode Together

These three measures answer slightly different questions.

| Measure    | Main Question                     | Key Characteristic                        |
| ---------- | --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| **Mean**   | What is the mathematical average? | Sensitive to extreme values               |
| **Median** | What is the middle observation?   | More resistant to extreme values          |
| **Mode**   | What occurs most often?           | Useful for categories and repeated values |

### Consultant question

Don't automatically ask:

> "What is the average?"

Ask:

> **"Which measure of center best represents the business question and the shape of the data?"**

---

# 11. Dispersion: How Spread Out Is the Data?

Now that we understand the center, the next question is:

> **How much do the observations differ from each other?**

This is the idea of **dispersion**.

Two datasets can have the same mean but very different behavior. A data story becomes much more useful when we know how tightly or loosely the values are spread.

# 12. Measures of Spread

Once we understand the center of the data, we also need to quantify how much the values move around that center.

This is where measures of **dispersion** become important.

Consider two delivery regions:

### Region A

```text
28, 29, 30, 30, 31, 32
```

### Region B

```text
10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60
```

Both have:

**Mean = 30**

But the business experience is very different. Region A is stable, while Region B is highly variable.

> **Dispersion describes how widely the data values are spread around the center.**

---

## Range

The simplest measure of dispersion is the:

> **Range**

### Formula

```text
Range = Maximum − Minimum
```

For Region A:

```text
32 − 28 = 4 minutes
```

For Region B:

```text
60 − 10 = 50 minutes
```

The range immediately shows us that Region B is much more variable.

### Limitation

The range uses only two observations:

* Minimum
* Maximum

Therefore, one extreme outlier can dramatically change the range.

---

## Interquartile Range — IQR

The **Interquartile Range (IQR)** focuses on the middle 50% of observations.

It is calculated as:

> **IQR = Q3 − Q1**

where:

* **Q1** = first quartile
* **Q3** = third quartile

Conceptually:

```text
Minimum       Q1        Median        Q3       Maximum
  |------------|-----------|------------|-----------|
               <----------->
                 Middle 50%
                    = IQR
```

Why is this useful?

Because the IQR is less affected by extreme values.

If a dataset contains a few unusually large or small observations, the IQR can provide a better representation of the **core spread of the data** than the range.

---

## Quartiles

Quartiles divide ordered data into four approximately equal parts.

```text
0%          25%          50%          75%          100%
|------------|------------|------------|------------|
             Q1          Median        Q3
```

Therefore:

* Q1 = 25th percentile
* Median = 50th percentile
* Q3 = 75th percentile

The middle 50% of observations lie between Q1 and Q3.

That interval is the IQR.

---

## Standard Deviation

The standard deviation is another measure of dispersion.

It tells us, broadly:

> **How far observations tend to vary from the mean.**

A **small standard deviation** means observations tend to be close to the mean.

A **large standard deviation** means observations tend to be more spread out.

For example:

### Store A

Average revenue = ₹10 lakh

Standard deviation = ₹0.5 lakh

### Store B

Average revenue = ₹10 lakh

Standard deviation = ₹4 lakh

Both stores have the same average revenue.

But Store B has much greater variability.

That difference may matter to the business.

---

## Variance vs Standard Deviation

Variance and standard deviation are closely related.

### Variance

Variance measures the average squared deviation from the mean.

### Standard deviation

Standard deviation is the square root of variance.

```text
Variance
    ↓ square root
Standard Deviation
```

Why is standard deviation often easier to interpret?

Because it is expressed in the **same units as the original data**.

If delivery time is measured in minutes:

> Standard deviation is measured in minutes.

Variance is expressed in squared units.

For introductory data analysis, focus on the interpretation rather than memorizing the formula.

---

## Comparing the Measures of Dispersion

| Measure                | What It Tells You                            | Main Limitation                  |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| **Range**              | Distance between minimum and maximum         | Very sensitive to extreme values |
| **IQR**                | Spread of the middle 50%                     | Does not use all observations    |
| **Variance**           | Overall variability using squared deviations | Units are squared                |
| **Standard Deviation** | Typical amount of variation around the mean  | Sensitive to extreme values      |

### Consultant perspective

Ask:

> **"Which measure best represents the variability relevant to my business question?"**

There is no single dispersion measure that is always best.

---

## Outliers

An **outlier** is an observation that is unusually far from the rest of the data.

Imagine delivery times:

```text
22, 24, 25, 27, 28, 30, 31, 32, 35, 120
```

The 120-minute delivery is very different from the others.

It may be an outlier.

But an important point is:

> **An outlier is not automatically an error.**

It could represent:

* A data-entry mistake
* A system problem
* An unusual customer
* A genuine business event
* A rare operational failure

Therefore:

> **Do not automatically delete outliers. Investigate them first.**

---

## Why Outliers Matter

Outliers can affect:

* Mean
* Range
* Variance
* Standard deviation
* Some statistical models

They may also contain valuable business information.

For example:

If most deliveries take 20–40 minutes but a small number take over 2 hours, those extreme observations may reveal an important operational problem.

A consultant should ask:

> **"Why are these observations unusual?"**

rather than:

> **"How do I remove them?"**

---

# 13. Correlation and Association

Now let's ask a different question.

Suppose we have data on:

* Distance from restaurant
* Delivery time

We want to know:

> **Do longer delivery distances tend to be associated with longer delivery times?**

This introduces the concept of **correlation**.

Correlation describes the **direction and strength of a linear relationship between two numerical variables**.

---

## Positive Correlation

Suppose:

> As distance increases, delivery time tends to increase.

Conceptually:

```text
Delivery Time
     ↑
     │              ●
     │           ●
     │         ●
     │      ●
     │    ●
     │  ●
     │ ●
     └────────────────────→
             Distance
```

This is a **positive relationship**.

As one variable increases, the other tends to increase.

Examples:

* Advertising spend ↔ sales
* Distance ↔ delivery time
* Experience ↔ salary

The relationship does not have to be perfect.

---

## Negative Correlation

Suppose:

> As a discount increases, profit margin tends to decrease.

Conceptually:

```text
Profit Margin
     ↑
     │ ●
     │   ●
     │     ●
     │       ●
     │          ●
     │            ●
     │              ●
     └────────────────────→
             Discount
```

This is a **negative relationship**.

As one variable increases, the other tends to decrease.

---

## Weak or No Correlation

Sometimes two variables do not show a clear linear relationship.

```text
Delivery Time
     ↑
     │   ●       ●
     │      ●
     │ ●          ●
     │        ●
     │    ●
     │          ●
     │ ●
     └────────────────────→
             Distance
```

The points are scattered without a clear linear pattern.

This suggests a **weak linear relationship**.

---

## Strong vs Weak Correlation

Correlation is not only about direction.

It is also about **strength**.

### Strong positive correlation

```text
     ●
    ●
   ●
  ●
 ●
```

The points closely follow an upward pattern.

### Weak positive correlation

```text
  ●     ●
     ●
●       ●
   ●
      ●
```

There is an upward tendency, but the points are more scattered.

### Important

A correlation can be:

* Positive
* Negative
* Weak
* Strong
* Close to zero

---

## Correlation Does Not Mean Causation

This is one of the most important concepts in data analysis.

Suppose we discover:

> Ice cream sales increase when drowning incidents increase.

There may be a positive correlation.

Does eating ice cream cause drowning?

No.

A third variable — **hot weather** — may influence both.

Similarly, if we find:

> Delivery distance and delivery time are positively correlated.

That does not automatically prove distance is the only cause of longer delivery times.

Other factors could include:

* Traffic
* Weather
* Number of orders
* Restaurant preparation time
* Driver availability

Therefore:

> **Correlation identifies a relationship. It does not, by itself, establish causation.**

---

## Correlation Coefficient

A commonly used measure of linear correlation is the **Pearson correlation coefficient**, usually represented by:

> **r**

Its value lies between:

```text
-1  ←──────────  0  ──────────→  +1
Strong           No              Strong
negative         linear           positive
                 correlation
```

Conceptually:

|     Correlation | Interpretation                       |
| --------------: | ------------------------------------ |
|          **+1** | Perfect positive linear relationship |
| **Close to +1** | Strong positive linear relationship  |
|  **Close to 0** | Weak or no linear relationship       |
| **Close to -1** | Strong negative linear relationship  |
|          **-1** | Perfect negative linear relationship |

For this introductory course, focus on interpretation rather than calculation.

---

## Be Careful With Correlation

Correlation has limitations.

A correlation close to zero does not necessarily mean that two variables have **no relationship at all**.

They may have a **non-linear relationship**.

For example:

```text
Y
↑
│ ●               ●
│   ●           ●
│     ●       ●
│       ●   ●
│        ●
└────────────────────→ X
```

The variables clearly have a pattern, but it is not a straight-line relationship.

Therefore:

> **Pearson correlation measures linear association.**

Always visualize the data when possible.

---

# 14. From Statistics to Business Insight

Now return to our food delivery case.

Suppose our analysis shows:

### Center

Median delivery time:

**34 minutes**

### Spread

IQR:

**12 minutes**

### Distribution

Right-skewed.

### Outliers

A small number of deliveries take more than:

**90 minutes**

### Correlation

Distance and delivery time:

**Positive correlation**

Now we can begin translating statistics into business language.

Instead of saying:

> "The distribution is positively skewed and has a high IQR."

A consultant might say:

> **"Most deliveries are completed within a relatively narrow time range, but a small number of significantly delayed orders are pulling the average delivery time upward. Longer delivery distances are also associated with longer delivery times."**

That is much more useful to a business stakeholder.

---

## Statistical Summary vs Business Interpretation

Consider the following:

| Statistical Finding                | Possible Business Interpretation                                             |
| ---------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Mean delivery time = 38 min        | Average operational delivery time is 38 minutes                              |
| Median = 34 min                    | A typical order is delivered in around 34 minutes                            |
| Mean > Median                      | A small number of very long deliveries may be pulling the average upward     |
| High IQR                           | Delivery experience varies considerably                                      |
| Large standard deviation           | Delivery times are inconsistent                                              |
| Right skew                         | Most deliveries are relatively quick, but some experience significant delays |
| Outliers above 90 min              | Investigate severe delivery failures                                         |
| Positive distance-time correlation | Longer delivery distances tend to be associated with longer delivery times   |
| Multiple peaks                     | Different operational groups or customer segments may exist                  |

This is the skill we ultimately want to develop:

> **Translate statistical patterns into business meaning.**

---

## A Simple Consultant Framework

When looking at a new numerical dataset, use this sequence:

### Step 1 — Center

> What is typical?

Look at:

**Mean → Median → Mode**

### Step 2 — Spread

> How variable is the data?

Look at:

**Range → IQR → Standard Deviation**

### Step 3 — Shape

> What does the distribution look like?

Look for:

**Symmetry → Skewness → Peaks → Outliers**

### Step 4 — Relationships

> Are variables related?

Look at:

**Scatter plots → Correlation → Associations**

### Step 5 — Business Meaning

Ask:

> **So what does this mean for the business?**

This final step is what separates a data calculation from a data analysis.

## Consultant Challenge

You are given the following summary of a food delivery dataset:

```text
Number of orders: 50,000

Mean delivery time: 42 minutes
Median delivery time: 34 minutes
Minimum: 18 minutes
Maximum: 180 minutes
IQR: 11 minutes
Standard deviation: 21 minutes

Distribution: Right-skewed

Orders above 90 minutes: 2%
```

The operations manager says:

> **"Our average delivery time is 42 minutes. Therefore, most customers wait around 42 minutes."**

### Do you agree?

Think about:

* Mean vs median
* Right skewness
* Extreme values
* IQR
* Outliers

<details>
<summary>Suggested interpretation</summary>

Not necessarily.

The median is **34 minutes**, which means half of the observed orders are at or below 34 minutes and half are at or above 34 minutes.

The mean of 42 minutes is substantially higher than the median, and the distribution is right-skewed. This suggests that a relatively small number of very long deliveries are pulling the mean upward.

The 180-minute maximum also indicates the presence of extreme observations.

Therefore, saying that "most customers wait around 42 minutes" would be misleading.

A better statement would be:

> **"A typical delivery takes around 34 minutes, while a smaller number of very long deliveries increase the overall average to 42 minutes. These extreme delays should be investigated separately."**

</details>

## Final Takeaway

Descriptive statistics helps us turn a large collection of observations into a meaningful summary.

But remember:

> **One statistic is rarely enough.**

A good analyst looks at:

**Center**

→ What is typical?

**Spread**

→ How variable is it?

**Shape**

→ How is the data distributed?

**Outliers**

→ Are there unusual observations?

**Relationships**

→ Are variables associated?

**Business context**

→ Why does any of this matter?

The ultimate goal is not to produce a table containing mean, median, standard deviation, and correlation.

The goal is to move from:

> **Data → Pattern → Interpretation → Business Insight**

That is the foundation of effective exploratory data analysis.
