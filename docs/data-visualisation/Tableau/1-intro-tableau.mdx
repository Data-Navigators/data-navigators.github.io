# Introduction to Tableau

In the previous sections, we learned that good data visualisation does not begin with a chart.

It begins with a **business problem**.

The analyst first needs to understand:

- what the business is trying to achieve,
- what decision needs to be supported,
- what questions need to be answered,
- what data is required.

Only then should we decide how the analysis should be visualised.

In this section, we will apply this process using **Tableau**.

---

# The Analytical Workflow

Throughout the Tableau exercise, we will follow this process:

**Business Problem  
→ Business Decision  
→ Analytical Questions  
→ Data  
→ Data Preparation  
→ Metrics and Dimensions  
→ Analysis  
→ Visualisation  
→ Executive Dashboard  
→ Insight  
→ Recommendation**

The objective is not simply to learn how to create charts in Tableau.

The objective is to use Tableau as part of an analytical solution that supports better business decisions.

---

# What is Tableau?

Tableau is a business intelligence and data visualisation tool.

It allows analysts to:

- connect to different data sources,
- explore and analyse data,
- create calculated fields and business metrics,
- create visualisations,
- compare different groups and measures,
- identify trends and patterns,
- build interactive dashboards,
- apply filters and interactions,
- communicate findings to stakeholders.

For this exercise, we will use **Tableau Public**.

> **Important:** Work published to Tableau Public may be publicly accessible. Do not upload confidential or sensitive organisational data.

---

# How Tableau Organises Your Work

Before working with the case study, it is useful to understand the main ways Tableau organises visualisations.

---

## Worksheet

A **worksheet** is where you create an individual visualisation.

For example, a worksheet could contain:

- a bar chart,
- a line chart,
- a scatter plot,
- a table,
- a KPI.

A worksheet normally focuses on answering **one analytical question**.

---

## Dashboard

A **dashboard** combines several worksheets into one view.

A dashboard may contain:

- multiple visualisations,
- KPI cards,
- filters,
- legends,
- text,
- interactive elements.

The purpose of a dashboard is to bring related analytical findings together so that the audience can understand a larger business problem.

---

## Story

A Tableau **Story** allows multiple worksheets or dashboards to be presented in a sequence.

This can be useful when an analyst wants to guide the audience through several stages of an analysis.

For example:

**Overview  
→ Identify an Important Segment  
→ Investigate Further  
→ Recommendation**

In this exercise, our main focus will be on creating an **executive dashboard**.

# The Case Study: Travel Insurance

We will apply the Tableau workflow using a travel insurance dataset.

Imagine a company that facilitates the sale of travel insurance through different agencies and distribution channels.

The company offers different insurance products for travellers going to destinations around the world.

Management wants to better understand the performance of the travel insurance portfolio and identify where opportunities or risks may exist.

We will eventually use the data to investigate questions such as:

- Which products generate the strongest sales?
- Which channels contribute most to the business?
- Which destinations generate high sales?
- Are some destinations associated with higher Claim Rates?
- Where should management investigate further?

Before answering these questions, however, we first need to understand and prepare the data.

# Understanding Dimensions and Measures

Now that we know what the dataset contains, we can introduce two important Tableau concepts:

**Dimensions** and **Measures**.

---

## Dimensions

Dimensions describe or categorise the data.

They normally answer:

> **How do we want to group or break down the analysis?**

Examples from our travel insurance dataset include:

- Agency
- Agency Type
- Distribution Channel
- Product Name
- Claim
- Destination
- Gender

For example, if we want to compare performance across different products:

`Product Name`

would be a dimension.

---

## Measures

Measures are numerical values that can usually be aggregated.

They answer:

> **What are we measuring?**

Examples from the travel insurance dataset include:

- Duration
- Net Sales
- Commission
- Age

For example, if we want to measure the total value of travel insurance sales:

`Net Sales`

would be a measure.

---

# Dimensions and Measures Work Together

Most analytical questions require both a measure and a dimension.

Consider:

> **Which travel insurance products generate the highest sales?**

We need:

**Measure:** Net Sales

and:

**Dimension:** Product Name

Together:

**Net Sales by Product Name**

This could then be visualised to help compare product performance.

---

## Another Example

Consider:

> **Which destinations have higher Claim Rates?**

We need:

**Measure:** Claim Rate

and:

**Dimension:** Destination

Together:

**Claim Rate by Destination**

Notice that `Claim Rate` does not exist directly in the original dataset.

It will need to be calculated from the available data.

This is why understanding and preparing the dataset comes before building the dashboard.

---

# From Raw Data to Business Metrics

The original dataset contains raw fields.

However, stakeholders often think in terms of business metrics rather than raw columns.

For example, management may want to know:

- How many policies were sold?
- What was Total Net Sales?
- What was the Claim Rate?
- How did performance differ across products or markets?

Some of these measures will need to be created from the raw data.

# From Dashboard to Decision

Creating the dashboard is not the final objective.

You will also use the analysis to communicate:

1. **What happened?**
2. **Where is the important opportunity or risk?**
3. **Why does it matter?**
4. **What should management investigate or consider doing next?**

The final output should therefore move beyond:

> **"Here is my dashboard."**

towards:

> **"Here is what the data tells us and why it matters to the business."**

# Key Takeaway

Tableau is a **tool** within the analytical process.

Do not begin by asking:

> **"Which chart should I create?"**

Begin by asking:

> **"What business question am I trying to answer?"**

Then use the data and the appropriate visualisation to help answer that question.
