What is Data Visualisation?

Data visualisation is the process of presenting data in a visual form so that information can be understood more easily.

In data analytics, however, visualisation is not simply about making data look attractive.

Its purpose is to help people:

  • understand what is happening,
  • identify important patterns or changes,
  • focus attention on what matters,
  • communicate findings clearly, and
  • support better decisions.

A good visualisation should make data easier to understand and help the audience answer a question.

Why Do We Visualise Data?​

Imagine receiving a spreadsheet containing thousands of rows of data.

The data may contain everything required to answer a business question, but it can still be difficult to understand what is important.

A stakeholder may want to know:

  • Is business performance improving or deteriorating?
  • Is there an unusual change that requires attention?
  • Which areas are performing well or poorly?
  • Are there patterns in the data that should be investigated?
  • Is there a problem that requires action?

Looking through hundreds or thousands of rows makes these questions difficult to answer quickly.

Visualisation helps organise and present data so that important patterns, differences and changes are easier to recognise.

Instead of asking stakeholders to examine all the raw data themselves, an analyst helps them focus on the information that matters.

The objective is therefore not simply to display data.

It is to help the audience move from:

Data → Understanding → Insight → Decision → Action

From Data to Decision​

Consider the following customer support data:

MonthTickets ReceivedTickets Resolved
June181161
July123132
August202160

These numbers tell us what happened.

However, an analyst should go further and ask:

What should the stakeholder notice?

In August, there is a much larger difference between tickets received and tickets resolved.

This raises an important business question:

Is the support team struggling to keep up with incoming demand?

That question may then lead to further analysis before management decides what action to take.

This illustrates the role of visualisation in analytics.

Visualisation helps make important information easier to recognise, communicate and investigate.

A Chart Is Not the Final Outcome​

Creating a chart is not the final goal of data analysis.

The analyst ultimately wants to help the stakeholder understand what the data means.

A useful way to think about this is:

Data → Information → Insight → Decision → Action

Data​

Data consists of raw observations or measurements.

For example:

202 tickets were received in August and 160 were resolved.

Information​

Information provides meaning or context to the data.

For example:

More tickets were received than resolved in August.

Insight​

An insight explains why the information matters.

For example:

The gap between incoming and resolved tickets widened significantly in August, suggesting that support demand may have exceeded processing capacity.

Decision​

The stakeholder may then need to make a decision.

For example:

Does the organisation need additional support capacity, or is a particular issue causing the increase in tickets?

Action​

Further analysis may result in an action.

For example:

Investigate which types of tickets contributed most to the increase before deciding whether additional staffing is required.

The chart helps communicate the analysis, but the real objective is to support understanding and decision making.

Start With the Question, Not the Visual

A common mistake in data visualisation is to begin by asking:

"What should I put on my dashboard?"

or:

"What chart should I create?"

A better starting point is:

"What are we trying to understand?"

and:

"What decision are we trying to support?"

Consider this request from a stakeholder:

"Our customer support team seems overwhelmed lately. Customers are waiting longer for their problems to be resolved. Can you help us understand what is happening?"

The analyst should not immediately begin building a dashboard.

First, the analyst needs to understand:

  1. What is the actual business problem?
  2. What does the stakeholder want to know?
  3. What decision might be made?
  4. What information would help support that decision?
  5. What data is available?

Only after these questions have been considered should the analyst decide how the information should be visualised.

Know Your Audience

The same data may need to be presented differently to different people.

Before designing any visualisation, ask:

Who is going to use this information?

Different audiences have different needs.

Executive Audience​

Senior management usually wants to understand:

  • overall business performance,
  • major changes,
  • risks and opportunities,
  • areas requiring attention,
  • recommended actions.

Executives usually do not need every detail contained in the dataset.

They need enough information to understand the situation and make a decision.

Operational Audience​

Operational teams may require more detailed information.

They may need:

  • individual records,
  • frequent updates,
  • detailed breakdowns,
  • filters,
  • the ability to investigate specific cases.

For example, a customer support manager may need to identify exactly which tickets have already breached their service target.

Analytical Audience​

Analysts may need even more detail so that they can:

  • explore the data,
  • compare different groups,
  • investigate unusual patterns,
  • test possible explanations,
  • perform further analysis.

A dashboard designed for an analyst may therefore be too complicated for an executive audience.

Visualisation as Communication

Data visualisation is also a form of communication.

A stakeholder should not need to study a dashboard for a long time before understanding its purpose.

A useful way to communicate an analytical finding is:

Context → Finding → Why It Matters → Action

Context​

What is the business situation?

Customer support management is concerned about increasing waiting times.

Finding​

What does the analysis show?

Incoming ticket demand has begun to exceed the number of tickets being resolved.

Why It Matters​

What is the potential business impact?

If the pattern continues, unresolved tickets may accumulate and customer waiting times may increase.

Action​

What should happen next?

Investigate the source of the increase before deciding whether additional support capacity is required.

This simple structure will be used again later when we discuss data storytelling.

What Makes a Useful Visualisation?

A useful visualisation should help the audience:

Understand​

What is happening?

Compare​

How do different groups, periods or measures differ?

Identify​

What requires attention?

Investigate​

Where should we look more closely?

Decide​

What should we do next?

The objective is not to include as much information as possible.

The objective is to include the information that helps answer the business question.

Questions to Ask Before Creating a Visualisation

Before creating any visualisation, ask:

QuestionWhy It Matters
What are we trying to understand?Defines the purpose
What is the business problem?Keeps the analysis relevant
Who is the audience?Determines the level of detail
What decision might be made?Connects the analysis to action
What information would help?Guides the analysis
What data is available?Determines what can be investigated
What should the audience understand afterwards?Defines the key message

Key Takeaways

Data visualisation is not simply about creating charts.

It helps people turn data into understanding.

A good visualisation should:

  1. start with a question,
  2. focus on what matters,
  3. consider the audience,
  4. communicate findings clearly, and
  5. support decision making.

Remember:

Data → Understanding → Insight → Decision → Action
